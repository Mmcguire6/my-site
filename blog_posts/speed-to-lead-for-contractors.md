A homeowner fills out your contact form at 7:40 p.m. and three other contractors' forms right after it. Drift tested 433 companies' response times to a fresh web lead and found that only 7% replied within five minutes, and 55% never replied at all within five business days ([Drift](https://medium.com/marketing-102/we-tested-the-response-times-of-433-sales-teams-here-are-the-results-ef2fd005072e)). Whoever answers first usually gets the job, and most businesses aren't even in the running.

This isn't the same leak as a missed phone call or a quote that went quiet for a week. It's the moment right after someone submits your form, and most of that moment gets wasted. Here are five reasons it happens, and how to fix each one.

## 1. The lead sits unseen for hours

**The pain:** A form submission lands in an inbox the owner checks twice a day, or a notification gets buried under invoices and supplier emails. Research on business lead response compiled by RevenueHero found the average company takes roughly 29 hours to reply to a new lead, and more than six in ten never reply at all. By the time anyone opens that email, the homeowner has already booked someone else.

**The fix:** Route every new lead to a channel you actually check in real time, not just an inbox: a text to your phone, not only an email you'll read tonight. If you can't see it the moment it lands, you can't be fast about it.

## 2. You're up a ladder, not at a keyboard

**The pain:** Alberta trades don't work from a desk. You're on a roof in Edmonton, under a sink in Red Deer, or driving between job sites, and a new lead showing up on your phone doesn't mean you can stop and type a reply. The gap between "the lead arrived" and "you had a free hand" is where the job slips away.

**The fix:** Separate the acknowledgment from the full reply. An instant, automatic response ("Thanks, we got your request — we'll call you within the hour") buys you the time to actually finish the job you're on, without the homeowner assuming you ignored them.

## 3. "We'll get back to you" doesn't actually set an expectation

**The pain:** A generic auto-reply that just says thanks and nothing else leaves the homeowner guessing whether that means ten minutes or three days. An analysis of more than 15,000 web leads, later covered by Harvard Business Review, found that leads contacted within 5 minutes were roughly 21 times more likely to turn into a real sales conversation than leads contacted 30 minutes later ([Harvard Business Review / BYU](https://scholarsarchive.byu.edu/facpub/9711)). A vague reply doesn't buy you that window; it just delays the moment they give up and call the next name.

**The fix:** Tell the lead exactly what happens next and roughly when ("We'll call you within the hour to book your estimate"), and then actually hit that window. A specific promise you keep beats a polite one you don't.

## 4. Every lead gets treated the same, win or lose

**The pain:** A quote request for a $400 repair and one for a $15,000 roof replacement often land in the same inbox and get the same "I'll call back later" treatment. Nothing flags which leads are actually time-sensitive, so the big job that needed a same-day call gets the same lazy response as everything else, and it's the one most worth losing.

**The fix:** Route high-value or urgent requests (the words "emergency," "today," or a large job type) for an immediate call, not just a text. Not every lead deserves the same five minutes, but the ones that do should never wait.

## 5. There's no record of who got answered fast and who didn't

**The pain:** Without a system tracking it, speed-to-lead quietly erodes as you get busy. You might reply in two minutes on a quiet Tuesday and not at all on a Saturday with four jobs running, and you'd have no way of knowing it was happening, let alone which leads it cost you.

**The fix:** A simple log of when each lead came in and when it was first answered turns a gut feeling into a number you can actually manage. What you don't track, you can't fix.

## How this is different from a missed call or a cold quote

Speed to lead is its own leak, separate from the other two places jobs go missing. A [missed call](stop-losing-jobs-to-missed-calls.html) is a phone ringing out while you're on the job. A stalled quote is a job you already bid that needs a [follow-up](quote-follow-up-for-contractors.html) days or weeks later. Speed to lead is the few minutes right after a brand-new inquiry lands on your site, before any of that, and it's the easiest one to lose without ever noticing.

## Frequently asked questions

### How fast should I respond to a new website lead?

Within five minutes if you can manage it. Drift's test of 433 companies found only 7% hit that mark, and research built on an analysis of over 15,000 web leads (later covered by Harvard Business Review) found leads contacted within 5 minutes were roughly 21 times more likely to turn into a real conversation than ones contacted 30 minutes later. Even a quick automatic acknowledgment that sets a clear timeframe is far better than silence.

### What is "speed to lead" and why does it matter for contractors?

Speed to lead is how fast you respond to a brand-new inquiry, usually a web form or quote request, not a phone call or an old quote. It matters because Alberta homeowners typically request quotes from several contractors at once, and the first one to reply with a clear next step usually wins the job, regardless of who'd have done the better work.

### What's the difference between speed to lead and missed-call text-back?

Missed-call text-back covers a phone call that rang out while you were on the job. Speed to lead covers a web form or quote request that arrived and sat unanswered, which is a different moment in the funnel and needs a different fix: fast routing and an immediate reply, not a callback.

### Can I automate a fast response to web leads?

Yes, and it's the most reliable fix. An automatic text or email the instant a lead comes in, stating a clear timeframe for a real reply, closes most of the gap even before you've picked up the phone. The system does the part that has to happen in minutes; you handle the part that needs a real conversation.

### Is slow lead response really costing me jobs, or is it just marketing hype?

It's measurable. Drift's study found 55% of the companies it tested never responded to a test lead within five business days, and separate research puts the average business response time at roughly 29 hours. Every one of those hours is a window where a competitor with a faster reply can pick up the job instead.
