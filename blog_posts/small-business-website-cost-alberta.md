Ask what a small-business website costs and you'll get a range so wide it's useless: anywhere from free to tens of thousands of dollars. Technically true, and completely unhelpful when you're just trying to figure out what's fair to pay for your shop, trade, or clinic here in Alberta.

So let's make it useful. Here's what you're actually paying for by route, what drives the number up or down, and the part nobody warns you about: where the money quietly leaks after launch.

## What you'll pay, by route

There are really four ways to get a website, and they land at very different prices:

- **Do it yourself on a builder (Wix, Squarespace, GoDaddy):** a monthly or yearly subscription, and the cheapest route on paper. You supply all the time, the design, and the copy, and these platforms are often slow and hard to rank.
- **Hire a freelancer:** usually a one-time fee of a few thousand dollars and up for a custom small-business site. Quality swings wildly with the person.
- **Hire an agency:** usually the priciest route, with strategy, custom design, and development rolled into a bill that can run well past what a freelancer charges. Great work at the top end; a lot of markup and bloat at the low end.
- **Managed monthly (design-build-host on a subscription):** one predictable monthly bill that rolls hosting, updates, and support together instead of a big upfront hit. That's how we work: our Base System is **$97/month**, with the website built free.

If you're paying upfront for a professional build, the number swings hard based on what's actually in the site.

## What actually drives the price

Two "five-page websites" can come back with very different quotes depending on:

- **Custom design vs. template.** A tuned, on-brand design costs more than dropping your logo into a stock theme, and usually converts better.
- **Number of pages.** A five-page brochure site is a fraction of a 30-page site with a page for every service and town you serve (the kind that actually [ranks and pulls leads](contractor-website-not-getting-leads.html)).
- **Functionality.** Online booking, payments, a customer login, or e-commerce each add real build time.
- **Copywriting and photos.** Words that sell and real photos of your work are often the difference between a site that converts and one that just sits there, and they cost either your time or someone's fee.
- **Speed and SEO done right.** A site built to [load fast](why-is-my-website-so-slow.html) and rank locally takes more skill than one thrown together in a page builder.

## The costs nobody warns you about

Here's the part that ambushes owners: the sticker price isn't the real price. The ongoing costs most people overlook can add up fast:

- Hosting: anywhere from a few dollars a month to a lot more for managed plans.
- Domain and SSL: small, but recurring.
- Plugins and subscriptions: booking tools, security, backups. The "it's only a few bucks a month" add-ons that stack up.
- Maintenance: updates, fixes, and the inevitable "can you change this" that a freelancer bills hourly for.

A "cheap" site with a pile of hidden upkeep isn't cheap. Always ask what the **total** looks like over two or three years, not just the build fee.

## The trap at both ends

The two most expensive mistakes are at opposite ends of the price range:

- **Too cheap.** A free DIY site that's slow, generic, and never ranks doesn't save you money. It costs you every lead it fails to capture. The cheapest site is the most expensive one if it brings in nothing.
- **Overpaying for bloat.** On the other end, some agencies charge a premium for a site built on the same heavy page-builder you could rent yourself for a small monthly fee. You're paying top dollar for something that's slow by design.

The goal isn't the lowest price or the highest. It's the best **return:** a fast, clear, well-ranked site that brings in more work than it costs, without a five-figure bill or a pile of hidden fees.

It ranges from a cheap DIY subscription to a big agency invoice, and most of the difference comes down to custom design, number of pages, functionality, and whether speed and SEO are done properly. ### Why are website quotes so different for the same thing?

Because "a website" can mean wildly different builds. A templated five-page site and a custom, fast, 30-page site engineered to rank locally are different amounts of work, even if both are "a website." Quotes also vary by who does it: a solo freelancer, a boutique studio, or a big agency with overhead and markup. Always compare what's actually included: design, pages, copy, speed, SEO, and ongoing support.

### What are the ongoing costs of a website?

Beyond the build, plan for hosting, domain and SSL renewals, plugin and tool subscriptions, security, backups, and maintenance. These are the costs most owners forget, and they can quietly make a "cheap" site the expensive one. Ask any provider for the total cost over two to three years, not just the upfront fee.

### Is a cheap DIY website worth it?

It can work for a very simple presence, but for a business that needs leads it usually costs more than it saves. DIY builders tend to be slow, hard to rank, and generic, and a site that never brings in work is the most expensive option there is, no matter how little you paid for it. If the site is meant to generate customers, pay for speed, clarity, and local SEO.
