Roughly **92% of people read reviews before they ever call a contractor** — and it only takes **1 to 3 bad ones to turn away around two-thirds of them.** Your reviews aren't a nice-to-have next to your website. For a lot of homeowners, they're the whole decision.

So if the phone's gone quiet and you can't figure out why, don't stop at your website and your Google Maps ranking. Pull up your reviews the way a stranger would, and read them cold. Here are five reasons what they see there is costing you jobs — and how to fix each one.

## 1. One bad review does more damage than you think

**The pain:** You did 40 good jobs this year and one homeowner had a bad day. That shouldn't define you — except it often does. Data from contractor-focused research puts it plainly: **just 1 to 3 negative reviews are enough to turn away roughly 67% of potential customers**, regardless of how many good ones sit above them. A single bad review near the top of your profile can outweigh a dozen good ones a scroll below it.

**The fix:** Don't panic-request a takedown Google almost never grants, and don't ignore it and hope it sinks. Respond calmly and specifically (more on that below), and — most importantly — make sure it isn't sitting alone. A steady flow of recent good reviews pushes one bad one down the page and puts it in honest context instead of letting it stand as your headline.

## 2. Your star rating has a bar most customers won't let you clear — or miss

**The pain:** The bar keeps rising. As of 2026, roughly **68% of consumers say they won't consider a business rated under 4 stars** — up sharply from about 55% just a year earlier. If your average has drifted to a 3.8 from a few old, unanswered complaints, you're now below the line for a majority of the people searching, before they've read a single word about your work.

**The fix:** Check your actual average today, not the number you remember from a year ago. If it's under 4, the fastest lever isn't damage control on the old reviews — it's volume of new ones. A dozen fresh 5-star reviews move your average faster than any single review you try to get removed.

## 3. Silence on a bad review reads as guilt

**The pain:** Homeowners don't just read the review — they read what you did about it. Roughly **53% expect a business to reply to a negative review within a week**, and **97% actually read the responses** businesses leave. An unanswered bad review doesn't just sit there neutrally; to the next reader, silence looks like confirmation.

**The fix:** Reply within a few days, every time — calm, specific, and public. Acknowledge what happened, state what you did or offered to fix it, and leave the anger out of it, even if the review wasn't fair. You're not really writing to the unhappy customer at that point. You're writing to the 50 future customers reading over their shoulder.

## 4. You're relying on memory to ask for reviews — and memory doesn't convert

**The pain:** Most happy customers would leave you a review if you asked at the right moment. Almost none of them think to do it on their own days later, after the truck's gone and the invoice is paid. If your review system is "hope they remember," you're leaving your best asset — the customers who'd rate you 5 stars — completely untapped, while the rare unhappy one always finds the time to complain.

**The fix:** Ask the moment the job wraps, not a week later. A short text with a one-tap review link, sent automatically the day the work is done, catches people while the good impression is still fresh — which is exactly [how we build review requests into every site](../features/review-automation.html), so it happens without you remembering to do it on a busy day.

## 5. Your best reviews never leave Google

**The pain:** You might have forty solid reviews sitting on your Google profile, and a visitor to your actual website would never know it. If your own site has no reviews on it — the same gap that [quietly costs contractor sites their leads](contractor-website-not-getting-leads.html) — a homeowner comparing you to two competitors sees zero proof at the exact moment they're deciding who to call.

**The fix:** Pull your best, most recent reviews onto your homepage and service pages, not just your Google listing. Reviews on Google win the click into your site; reviews *on* your site close the decision once they're there. It also feeds back into how you [rank on Google Maps](why-isnt-my-business-on-google-maps.html) in the first place — reviews are one of the strongest local ranking signals there is, so a system that keeps them coming helps you on both fronts at once.

## Where to start

In order of impact:

1. **Check your real average today** — know the number instead of guessing from memory.
2. **Reply to every negative review within a week** — calm, specific, public.
3. **Put a review ask on autopilot** — text it the day the job finishes, while the impression's still fresh.
4. **Feature reviews on your own website**, not just your Google profile.
5. **Keep the flow steady** — recent and frequent beats a big pile of old ones every time.

A few bad reviews aren't a crisis. A few bad reviews with no system behind them to outweigh them — that's the part actually costing you jobs.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many bad reviews does it take to lose customers?

It doesn't take many. Research from contractor-focused review studies shows that **just 1 to 3 negative reviews can turn away roughly 67% of potential customers**, even when plenty of good reviews sit alongside them. Recency and how you respond matter almost as much as the star rating itself — an old, unanswered complaint does more damage than a recent one you clearly resolved.

### Should I respond to negative reviews?

Yes, always, and within about a week. Roughly 53% of customers expect a reply in that window, and 97% read the responses businesses post. A calm, specific reply — what happened, what you did about it — reads as accountability to everyone who sees it after, even to the original reviewer: about 95% of customers say they'd do business again with a company that resolved their complaint quickly.

### How do I get more Google reviews for my small business?

The businesses that build up a steady flow of reviews almost always automate the ask instead of relying on memory. Text a short, one-tap review link the same day the job finishes, while the experience is still fresh — response rates drop fast the longer you wait. Asking in person works too, but it's easy to forget on a busy day, which is exactly why automating it matters.

### Can I get a bad review removed from Google?

Rarely, and only if it violates Google's policies (fake, spam, off-topic, or clearly abusive). A review that's simply negative but genuine almost never qualifies, no matter how unfair it feels. Your realistic lever isn't removal — it's a thoughtful public reply plus a steady stream of new reviews that puts the one bad one in honest context.

### How many reviews does a small business actually need?

There's no fixed number, but more and more recent beats fewer and older every time — both for the homeowners deciding whether to call and for your Google Maps ranking. Aim for a steady trickle of new reviews every month rather than a one-time push. A business with 60 reviews and a few from last month reads as more trustworthy than one with 200 reviews that stopped coming in two years ago.
