Only **19% of people think stock photography looks authentic** — yet **86% say authenticity is exactly what decides which businesses they trust enough to buy from.** If your website's hero image is a stranger in a hard hat giving a thumbs-up, you've planted a tiny credibility problem on the first thing every visitor sees.

It doesn't feel like a big deal. It is. Homeowners hiring a roofer, plumber, or electrician are trusting a stranger with their home, and trust is built or lost in the first few seconds on the page — long before they read a word of your copy. Here are five ways generic photos quietly cost Alberta trades jobs, and exactly what to swap in instead.

## 1. Your photos don't look like they're actually yours — and people can tell

**The pain:** Stock photography has a look: too-clean uniforms, too-white teeth, a job site with no dust on it. Homeowners have seen that same smiling technician on a dozen other company's websites, because they have — it's the same handful of licensed images recycled across the industry. Once a visitor clocks it as stock, every claim on the page gets a little less believable, because the one piece of "proof" on screen wasn't proof of anything.

**The fix:** Replace the stock hero image first — it's the highest-traffic real estate on the page. A photo of your actual truck, your actual crew, or the actual neighbourhood you work in (a quiet street in St. Albert, a new build in Airdrie) beats a polished stranger every time, even if it's a little less glossy.

## 2. No photos at all reads almost as badly as fake ones

**The pain:** Some owners skip the problem by just not using people photos — a logo, an icon, maybe a landscape shot. It doesn't help as much as you'd think. In a trust study of local business websites, sites with **no photos at all earned real trust from only 21% of people,** compared with **79% who trusted a site more when it showed a genuine owner or staff photo** instead.

**The fix:** A photo beats no photo almost every time, as long as it's real. You don't need a studio shoot — a decent phone photo of you or your crew, well-lit and in focus, clears the bar that an empty placeholder never will.

## 3. You're hiding the one photo that actually converts: you

**The pain:** Owners are often the most camera-shy people on the website, which is backwards — the founder's face is one of the strongest trust signals a small business has. In one direct test, swapping a stock image for a real photo of the business owner lifted sign-ups by **35%,** with nothing else on the page changed.

**The fix:** Put a real photo of yourself on the homepage or "About" page — not buried, not tiny. "Hi, I'm Dave, I've been fixing furnaces in Red Deer for 14 years" next to an actual photo of Dave does more selling than another paragraph of copy ever will.

## 4. Nobody can see the roof you replaced or the kitchen you renovated

**The pain:** A homeowner deciding between three contractors for a roof replacement in Edmonton isn't just checking if you're licensed — they're trying to picture the actual result. A page with zero real job photos gives them nothing to picture, so they click back to the competitor whose site is full of finished work.

**The fix:** Build a simple before-and-after gallery from jobs you've already done — a furnace swap, a bathroom reno, a new roof. These same photos do double duty on your Google Business Profile, where [local SEO](../features/local-seo.html) rewards businesses that post real, recent work over ones with a bare, generic listing.

## 5. Every trade site in town is wearing the same stock-photo uniform

**The pain:** When three roofers in the same city all use the same stock photo library, their websites start to blur together — same smiling technician, same generic van, same "quality you can trust" headline. A homeowner comparing quotes has no visual reason to remember you over the next name on the list, so the decision comes down to price alone.

**The fix:** Real photos are the cheapest differentiator you have. You don't need better branding or a lower price to stand out — you need to look like a specific, real business in a specific Alberta town, which a stock photo can never do and your own camera roll already can.

## Where to start

You don't need a professional photographer to fix this. In order of payoff:

1. **Swap the stock hero image first** — it's the first thing every visitor sees.
2. **Add one real photo of you or your crew** — it's the single highest-trust image on the page.
3. **Build a small gallery of real jobs** — even five or six phone photos of finished work.
4. **Keep it current** — a recent photo of last month's job beats a sharp photo from five years ago.

None of this requires a studio budget. A [fast, clear website](contractor-website-not-getting-leads.html) already does most of the work of turning visitors into calls — real photos are what makes a cautious homeowner believe what the page is telling them, the same way a steady stream of real photos on your [Google Business Profile](why-isnt-my-business-on-google-maps.html) builds trust before they ever click through.

## Frequently asked questions

### Do stock photos really hurt a small business website?

They can. Studies on brand authenticity find that only around 19% of people see stock photography as authentic, while the large majority say authenticity is what decides which businesses they trust. A stock photo doesn't make your site look unprofessional — it makes it look generic, and generic is the opposite of what gets a stranger to trust you with a job in their home.

### Is it better to have no photos than a bad stock photo?

Not really — both read poorly compared to a real one. Research on local business websites found sites with no photos at all earned real trust from only about 21% of visitors, versus roughly 79% who trusted a site more when it showed a genuine owner or staff photo. A simple, real photo almost always beats either having no photo or using a stock one.

### What kind of real photos should a contractor or local business use?

Three are worth prioritizing: a real photo of you or your team (the single highest-trust image you can use), recent photos of actual finished jobs, and a real shot of your vehicle or storefront. None of this needs a professional photographer — a clear, well-lit phone photo works, as long as it's genuinely yours.

### Will using real photos actually increase the leads I get?

It can move the needle more than people expect. In one direct test, replacing a stock image with a real photo of the business owner lifted conversions by 35% with nothing else on the page changed. Real photos won't fix a slow or confusing website on their own, but on a site that already works, they're one of the cheapest trust upgrades available.

### How many real photos does my website actually need?

You don't need dozens. A real photo of you or your crew, five or six photos of recent completed jobs, and a current shot of your vehicle or shopfront covers the most important trust signals. It's better to have a small gallery of genuinely recent work than a large one padded with old or unrelated photos.
