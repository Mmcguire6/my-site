You send the quote, the homeowner says "sounds good, let me think about it," and then — nothing. No callback, no "we went with someone else," just silence. So you shrug, assume they weren't serious, and move on to the next one.

They probably were serious. Research on sales follow-up puts the average deal at needing **5 or more follow-ups** to close, yet roughly **44% of salespeople give up after a single attempt** — and separate studies put the share who never even try a second touch even higher. Meanwhile, **66% of home-service businesses** say lead follow-up and conversion is one of their biggest challenges.

Put those together and it's not that the customer changed their mind. It's that you stopped talking to them before they were ready to say yes — and somewhere else, a competitor with a worse quote is still in the conversation. Here are five ways that gap costs Alberta trades real jobs, and how to close each one.

## 1. You quote once and call it done

**The pain:** Most estimates go out as a one-and-done event: send the PDF, wait for a reply. But roughly **80% of jobs take five or more follow-ups** before someone commits — they're comparing quotes, checking their budget, or just haven't gotten around to deciding yet. If your process stops after touch one, you're bailing out of most deals before the customer has even finished thinking.

**The fix:** Build a simple cadence instead of a single message: a same-day thank-you, a check-in around day 3, another around day 7 to 10, and a last "still want to move forward on this?" before you write it off. Four short touches, spread over two weeks, catches almost everyone who was ever going to say yes.

## 2. You wait too long after the estimate goes out

**The pain:** Speed matters here too, just later in the process than most owners think. Contractors who follow up within an hour of sending an estimate close **roughly 30 to 50% more jobs** than those who wait 48 hours or longer. Every day you wait is another day for a second and third quote to land in the same inbox — and price gets a lot harder to win on once you're the third name they've heard from, not the first.

**The fix:** Send a quick text or call the same day the quote goes out — not a hard sell, just "just sent that over, happy to answer anything." It costs two minutes and keeps you the freshest name in their head while the decision is still open.

## 3. You treat silence as a "no" — when it usually isn't

**The pain:** A quote that goes quiet feels like a rejection, so most owners leave it there. But industry data on "silent" quotes finds that **roughly 35 to 45% close after just one structured follow-up** sent 48 to 72 hours later. That's a huge share of "dead" jobs that were never actually dead — the customer just got busy, or was waiting to hear back from someone else and never did.

**The fix:** Make one follow-up on every quote non-negotiable, even the ones that feel gone. A short "wanted to check if you had any questions on the estimate" recovers a meaningful chunk of jobs you'd otherwise have written off for free.

## 4. Follow-up falls through the cracks between job sites

**The pain:** Knowing who got quoted, when, and what to say next is easy on paper and nearly impossible to actually run out of your head while you're on a roof, under a sink, or driving between three sites a day. This is exactly why follow-up is the challenge **66% of home-service businesses** name — not because they don't know it matters, but because nobody has time to chase a spreadsheet.

**The fix:** Put the follow-up on a schedule that runs itself. An automatic check-in text or email at day 1, day 3, and day 7 after every quote means the lead still gets chased even on your busiest week — it's the core of the [lead follow-up](../features/lead-follow-up.html) system we build into every site, working the same way it already does after [a missed call](stop-losing-jobs-to-missed-calls.html).

## 5. You worry follow-up feels pushy — so you under-do it

**The pain:** A lot of owners quietly under-follow-up because a second or third message feels like nagging a customer who already said no. But contractors who follow up consistently close **roughly 20 to 30% more jobs than those who don't**, with the same marketing spend and the same number of quotes going out. That gap isn't about being more aggressive — it's the jobs you were already earning, just walking away because nobody asked twice.

**The fix:** Reframe it as service, not pressure. "Just checking in — still happy to answer questions or get you booked in" reads as helpful, not desperate. Most homeowners got busy too; a polite nudge is usually welcome, not annoying.

## Where to start

You don't need a complicated system to fix this. In order of payoff:

1. **Follow up same day** — while your quote is still the freshest one in the inbox.
2. **Never leave a quote at one touch** — plan for at least three or four before you let it go.
3. **Always send one message on silent quotes** — it recovers more jobs than any other single fix here.
4. **Put the cadence on autopilot** — so it happens even on your busiest week.

A [website that brings in the lead](contractor-website-not-getting-leads.html) and a [system that answers the call](stop-losing-jobs-to-missed-calls.html) both get you to the quote. Following up is what turns that quote into a job — and it's the cheapest, least-technical fix on this whole list.

## Frequently asked questions

### How many times should I follow up on a quote before giving up?

Plan for at least three to four touches spread over about two weeks — a same-day thank-you, a check-in around day 3, another around day 7 to 10, and a final message before you close it out. Industry data shows most jobs that convert take five or more total touches, so stopping after one or two attempts means giving up on deals that were still very much alive.

### How soon should I follow up after sending an estimate?

Same day, ideally within an hour or two. Contractors who follow up that quickly close noticeably more jobs than those who wait 48 hours or longer, mostly because they're still the freshest quote in the customer's inbox instead of the third one competing for attention.

### What should I say when following up on a quote so I don't sound pushy?

Keep it short and helpful, not salesy: "Just checking in on the estimate I sent over — happy to answer any questions or get you booked in." It reads as service, not pressure. Most customers who've gone quiet just got busy, and a brief, friendly nudge is usually welcome.

### Is it worth following up if the customer never responded at all?

Yes — this is the highest-return message on the list. A meaningful share of quotes that go completely silent close after just one follow-up sent a couple of days later. Treating silence as a firm "no" throws away jobs that were simply waiting on a reminder.

### How do I keep track of quote follow-ups without a big CRM system?

For a handful of quotes a week, a simple shared calendar or spreadsheet with follow-up dates can work if someone actually checks it daily. Past that, an automatic system that sends scheduled check-ins after every quote removes the need to remember at all — which matters most on the weeks you're too busy to look at a spreadsheet in the first place.
