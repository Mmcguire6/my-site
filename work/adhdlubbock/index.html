№ 08 Frequently asked

Good to know

There are three types of ADHD: Attention-Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, Predominantly Hyperactive; Predominantly Inattentive; and Combined. The hyperactive type is commonly referred to as ADHD and the inattentive type is often referred to as ADD. “ADHD” is the term most often used when referring to any of the three types. ADHD is a neurogenetic condition that affects the executive functions of the brain.

To date there is no known cure — however, effective treatments include medications, counseling, developing coping skills, educating yourself and others, and having a positive attitude.

Avoiding too much sugar can help their minds be less scattered and minimize excessive bursts of energy followed by “sugar crashes.” Eating a high-protein diet can help energy, mood, and concentration levels stay more stabilized throughout the day. Caffeine will actually help them be more alert, focused, and calm — stimulants have a counterintuitive effect on people with ADHD. Exercise and adequate sleep are also critical parts of a good treatment plan.

The American Psychiatric Association's Diagnostic & Statistical Manual V (DSM-V) has a set of criteria that must be met. Health-care professionals often obtain information from the client, parents, teachers, etc. Several possible alternative causes of inattention or hyperactivity — such as anxiety or depression — must first be ruled out.

ADHD affects people socially, emotionally, and mentally — from keeping on task, to talking with a friend, to taking notes or even reading. Beyond the primary effects, there are secondary effects that often damage self-esteem, occurring when people with ADHD are misunderstood, picked on, bullied, or labeled as being “dumb,” lazy, or troublemakers. Sometimes self-fulfilling prophecies occur when children with ADHD believe the myths and labels people tell them.
